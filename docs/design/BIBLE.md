# WITCH YOU WERE HEAR

## Unified game concept and narrative bible

Supplied by the user on 2026-10-29 as the canon for the game. Kept as written; only the inline web thumbnails of the
source were dropped (the visual bar is `docs/art/references/`), and the source's citations of its own inputs are kept
as notes. Decisions that turn this bible into a build live in `docs/design/GAME_SPEC.md` and `docs/PLAN.md`.

Synthesis of the remembered project canon, narrative architecture, relational and puzzle spines, aesthetic direction,
and gameplay systems. Where earlier materials leave details open or potentially inconsistent, I identify the issue
rather than silently inventing canon.

The central idea I would preserve above everything else is this:

A witch who believes she must become extraordinary to avoid being replaced discovers that she can change the world,
influence other people, and even bring someone back—and still cannot make another person's choice mean what she wants
it to mean.

She does not need to stop being extraordinary. She needs to discover that her extraordinariness cannot guarantee a
relationship.

That distinction is the foundation of the story, the magic system, the puzzles, the protagonist's relationships, the
ending, and the name Witch you were hear.

# 1. The game in one page

TITLE

# Witch you were hear

A deliberately distorted echo of an intimate, familiar phrase. The spelling of "hear" opens the question of listening
and recognition; it should remain a suggestive layer, not a requirement that every scene decode the title literally.

GENRE

Relational Metroidbrainia Adventure

A mobile-first, fixed-camera 3D point-and-click adventure combining a mystery, environmental experimentation, a small
living town, and relationships that respond to what the player does and what other people believe those actions mean.

PLAYER FANTASY

Touch the impossible world. Discover how it thinks.

Begin as a spectacular witch who can make almost anything happen. Gradually discover that the world has its own
history, that other people have independent intentions, and that your actions create consequences you do not entirely
control.

EMOTIONAL PREMISE

A person tries to secure irreplaceability through extraordinary ability. When that fails, she must discover what
remains of a relationship after function, performance, certainty, and control can no longer guarantee it.

CORE EXPERIENCE

Explore → notice something strange → form a hypothesis → test it → observe a response → discover the limits of your
interpretation → try again with a better model.

## The public-facing pitch

Witch you were hear is a crooked, whimsical mystery about a red-haired witch whose magic can change almost
anything—except what another person chooses.

When someone she loves leaves for a mysterious place called the Fog, her magic begins to fail, and the little town
that once needed her starts finding other ways to get along. Determined to discover what happened and whether she can
bring him back, she descends into an impossible world beneath her home, where rooms obey discoverable rules, memories
become places, and the difference between a real event and what it proves becomes the hardest puzzle of all.

With the help of a very verbal raccoon, a collection of eccentric townspeople, and a world that refuses to stand still
for its protagonist, she must discover whether someone can matter without being indispensable.

The answer is not delivered in a final speech. It emerges through what she does when she can no longer be certain.

The game ends with an invitation, an impossible event, and a decision that does not wait for a complete explanation.

The player, meanwhile, is free to keep looking.

# 2. What the game is really about

The game has several connected themes, but they are not interchangeable. Their relationships are what make the
concept distinctive.

## The protagonist's central fear

Replacement.

Not merely being alone, being unwanted, or failing to be useful. She fears that she can be displaced—that someone
else can occupy her place once she ceases to be extraordinary enough.

## Her protective strategy

Become extraordinary enough to be irreplaceable.

Magic is a genuine talent and a source of delight. It also becomes a way of ensuring that people have reasons to keep
choosing her.

## The world's counterargument

A function can be replaced without a person being replaced.

The town can learn to perform tasks without her. Someone can leave because they want another life. A raccoon can
refuse a commission. None of these events automatically establishes what the people involved think of her or whether
she matters to them.

## The final discovery

Being loved is not the same as being necessary, and neither can be guaranteed by force.

She can still use magic. She can still help. She can still make extraordinary things. What changes is whether she
treats those abilities as an obligation she must fulfil to secure her place.

The protagonist's early history matters here. The remembered canon establishes that her father was once lovingly
present and later left. That earlier experience contributes to the fear that presence can become absence and that
someone who matters can be replaced.

The important restraint is that the game should not confirm her protective interpretation as an objective fact. The
father's departure must not secretly establish that she wasn't special enough. His reasons need not collapse into one
explanatory answer.

That is how the story avoids turning a complicated relationship into a simplistic origin story.

The player's own experience follows a parallel trajectory. At first, the player wants to solve the immediate problem.
Over time, they learn to distinguish what happened from what they believe happened, what another person needs from
what the player wants them to need, and what a successful action can establish from what it cannot.

The intended transformation is not from ignorance to omniscience. It is from rigid certainty to a more flexible
capacity to act.

This is the foundation of the game's relational and mythic design. (Source notes: RELATIONAL_SPINE.md,
MYTHIC_SPINE.md.)

## The distinctions the entire game must preserve

| Do not collapse these            | What the game needs to show                                                                            |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Replacement and changed function | Somebody else can do the witch's job without becoming the witch.                                       |
| Need and care                    | Someone can need her work without choosing her personally, and care without needing anything from her. |
| Desire and demand                | Wanting someone to stay does not give her ownership of their choice.                                   |
| Outcome and motive               | Getting the result does not prove why someone chose it.                                                |
| Capability and reliability       | Making one miraculous thing does not promise another.                                                  |
| Visibility and understanding     | Someone can look at her magic without understanding what she is trying to express.                     |
| Memory and presence              | Preserving the trace of a relationship does not preserve the relationship itself.                      |
| Access and certainty             | Being allowed into someone's world does not reveal everything about their mind.                        |
| Potential and permission         | Seeing how someone could improve does not authorize making them change.                                |
| Uncertainty and truth            | Some uncertainty comes from missing evidence; some remains even after diligent investigation.          |

These are not merely thematic statements to put in dialogue. They should create different responses, puzzle
solutions, spatial transformations, and lasting consequences.

# 3. The protagonist: a genuinely magical person, not a metaphor in a hat

## The Witch

Red hair · blue-grey eyes · violet hood · wand

She is funny, generous, dramatic, inventive, clever, socially magnetic, and genuinely capable of astonishing things.
She can also be arrogant, exhausting, impatient, or convinced that she already knows what would help.

Her characteristic declaration is:

"Watch this."

This character must be enjoyable before the player fully understands her.

She loves making things happen. Magic is tactile, funny, beautiful, and rewarding. She enjoys seeing a difficult
situation yield to an ingenious idea. She takes pleasure in the world responding to her touch.

Her defensive pattern emerges from those same genuine strengths. When a person pulls away, she does not necessarily
begin by expressing hurt. She tries to do something about the situation: investigate, fix, anticipate, protect,
repair, or produce something so impressive that nobody could reasonably want to do without her.

Her outward response to disappointment can be the Russian phrase:

«Ну и не надо».

It is not just sulking. It is a way of reclaiming interpretive control after something has hurt her: if she cannot
determine what the event means to the other person, she can declare what it means to her—or pretend it never
mattered.

Sometimes she becomes PROVING, demonstrating how remarkable she is. Sometimes she becomes HOUND, chasing an
explanation or trying to prevent a departure. Sometimes she becomes FINE, withdrawing behind a mask of indifference.
These are authored behavioural modes, not personality types presented to the player.

The game should never tell players that the witch is secretly bad, that her confidence is false, or that magic is
merely compensation for insecurity.

Her magic is real. Her generosity is real. Her longing is real. The problem is the conclusion that these good things
must continually produce proof of her right to be chosen.

### Her character arc

1. I can make it happen. The pleasure of agency. She touches the world and it changes.
2. I can make them stay. Capability becomes a strategy for controlling an uncertain relationship.
3. If they can do without me, I am replaceable. The town's adaptation threatens her interpretation of what matters.
4. I cannot make the choice mean what I need. She learns to distinguish real consequences from the conclusions she
   wants them to prove.
5. I can still choose what I do. Magic returns as agency rather than a guarantee of attachment.

The endpoint is not modesty, passivity, or a refusal to intervene. It is that she can say "I wanted you to stay"
without turning that wish into a demand, and can use her powers without making the result a contract the other person
never agreed to.

That preserves what is delightful about her while changing her relationship to it.

# 4. The world: a place that was already alive before the witch arrived

The setting should feel as though its residents have been doing strange, funny, occasionally incomprehensible things
for years. The witch is central to the player's experience, but she is not the sole source of the world's existence.

The town's defining quality is not random absurdity. It is absurdity with causal consistency.

A ridiculous object can exist for a ridiculous reason. A peculiar custom can solve a problem no outsider initially
recognizes. A character can make an unreasonable decision for a reason that becomes understandable later. A mechanism
can be visually nonsensical while obeying a discoverable rule.

This is the principle of safe, rule-governed stupidity: the player is free to be curious and try absurd things without
the world becoming arbitrary.

## The principal places

**The Town.** The living social world. Workshops, houses, public spaces, shared routines, odd local customs, and
people with incompatible preferences. The town changes as the witch loses and eventually regains her magic. Its most
important function is to demonstrate that the world continues whether or not she is in the room.

**The Original Garden.** A place the witch and First Love made together. It should contain both their tastes,
accidental growth, disagreements, imperfect compromises, and objects that make sense only when their shared history is
understood. At first it is simply a familiar place. Its importance grows as the player discovers how much of a
relationship cannot be preserved by remembering it.

**The Fog.** A beautiful, quiet, strangely relieving place that gradually draws First Love away. It offers a mode of
existence with less friction, uncertainty, pain, and certain ordinary obligations. It is not simply evil, and it should
not be exposed as a trick whose explanation invalidates the person who chooses it.

**The Basement / Underworld.** Initially a practical storage area full of old stock, forgotten equipment, materials,
tools, and obsolete magical objects. It gradually becomes impossible in depth, scale, and spatial logic. This is where
the game's environmental puzzles become puzzles about how the player models reality.

**The Garden Below.** A preserved reconstruction of the shared garden. It seems beautiful until the player notices
that the first love's preferences, disagreements, imperfections, and independent contributions have disappeared. It is
not a living shared space. It is the witch's preferred representation of one.

**The House.** The place that remains after the protagonist leaves. Its objects, gifts, traces, and unfinished
projects form a postgame archaeological space. By then the player knows enough to recognize relationships in the
residue of ordinary life, rather than treating everything as a disposable clue toward the next task.

These spaces should not merely represent emotional states. They should have their own physical histories and rules.
The garden becomes meaningful because the player investigates its actual differences. The Fog becomes meaningful
because the player observes what people do in relation to it. The basement changes because the player discovers how it
operates.

The world carries the meaning; the story does not have to explain every meaning aloud.

## The defining world transition

At the start, the player thinks the witch makes the world interesting.

By the end, the player understands that the witch has been participating in a world that was always more independent
than she imagined.

The change is subtle but fundamental:

- Initially, unusual events appear to happen because the witch acts.
- Later, events happen while she is absent, waiting, or occupied elsewhere.
- Finally, the player can leave the protagonist's immediate perspective and continue investigating a world that has
  not ceased to exist.

This is also why a small town is more suitable than an enormous kingdom. The game needs enough people and places for
relationships, objects, and histories to cross-reference one another. It does not need hundreds of locations that are
thematically interchangeable.

# 5. The central relationship: First Love and the Fog

The relationship with First Love is the main narrative pressure, and it must be more complicated than a missing-person
mystery.

The witch wants him to stay. He increasingly wants to spend time in the Fog. Neither desire is automatically
illegitimate.

The Fog's seduction happens incrementally:

1. He visits the Fog and returns.
2. He visits more frequently.
3. He begins concealing visits.
4. He stays longer.
5. His explanations become difficult to reconcile with his behaviour.
6. He becomes harder to reach.
7. He eventually leaves.

The final departure is almost administrative: the visible conclusion of a separation that has been happening for some
time.

This structure gives the player something more interesting than a single dramatic disappearance. They can observe a
process, form hypotheses about it, and discover that different observations do not necessarily establish one complete
explanation.

The witch initially interprets his departure as replacement. He experiences his movement toward the Fog as a choice.

That difference is critical.

The story must preserve the reality that he chose another world. The Fog may be strange, beautiful, limited, or
mysterious, but the narrative should not make it purely malevolent simply to protect the protagonist from the
implications of his decision.

Nor should the game reveal a secret explanation that makes his departure unquestionably correct or unquestionably
wrong.

His choices can be understood more accurately without becoming fully transparent.

## The crucial dramatic question

The witch's practical question is:

> Can I find a way to bring him back?

Her deeper question is:

> If I can make him return, how will I know that he chose to return?

Those are different questions with different solutions.

The first is an engineering problem within the world's magical rules.

The second concerns another person's freedom and what evidence can establish about it.

The game deliberately allows the first problem to have a solution while refusing to pretend that solving it
automatically answers the second. That is the foundation of the central "unsrescuable rescue" puzzle. (Source note:
MYTHIC_SPINE.md.)

# 6. The raccoon: the second mind who is not an accessory

## The Raccoon

A fully verbal, independent character with his own agenda, inventions, expectations, boundaries, and unfinished
projects.

He is not just a pet or a talking inventory assistant. He is another mind capable of misunderstanding the witch,
surprising her, leaving without prompting, and choosing when to share his discoveries.

The witch and raccoon have an exclusive telesomatic link. They can experience flashes of sensation, emotion, or
imagery from each other, but the connection transmits no semantic certainty.

A sensation does not arrive with a caption explaining what it means. An image is not a sentence. Emotional proximity
does not provide complete access to another person's interpretation.

The bond grants access, not certainty.

This is an ideal way to make the title's relationship between presence and hearing mechanically relevant: the
characters can be intimately connected and still misunderstand what the other is communicating.

## His own mirror arc

The raccoon is a parallel protagonist.

He can make astonishing inventions, but he fears what happens when people see one extraordinary result and decide that
it describes his normal level of ability.

His fear is not primarily endless work. It is the expectation that a peak performance has become his new standard.

He may build a miraculous prototype and then struggle to reproduce it. He might hide an invention because visibility
invites an assumption: if you can do this once, you can reliably do it whenever I ask.

His arc is about learning that:

- showing capability is not promising future capability;
- disappointment can be uncomfortable without destroying a relationship;
- an imperfect invention can be shown without being treated as a verdict on his worth;
- receiving help does not erase authorship;
- seeing how something could be improved does not grant permission to alter it.

The canonical progression is from hiding extraordinary results to showing an ordinary, mediocre one that he is unsure
about. The mediocre invention is the more intimate offering because it asks to be seen without the protection of a
masterpiece.

His workshop should contain roughly seventeen unfinished projects, with about three brought into meaningful focus. The
remainder make his mind visible without turning the whole workshop into a catalogue of quests.

The witch and raccoon ultimately learn related but distinct things. She must stop treating her capabilities as the
guarantee of her place. He must stop treating his capabilities as a promise to everyone who sees them.

They can influence each other without owning each other.

# 7. The town's characters and the distinctions they embody

These characters should not exist merely to teach the player lessons. Each has a concrete preference, a way of
behaving, a relationship to the witch, and a life that continues beyond the immediate puzzle.

Their narrative function emerges from the collision between what they want and what the witch assumes they want.

**Tomas — authorship.** Tomas does not reject assistance as such. He resists help that silently turns his problem into
somebody else's solution. The witch may repair his machine and receive gratitude, yet still damage his sense of
ownership. Or she may offer an option and let him decide whether to use it. His story teaches that help is a
relational action, not only a technical solution.

**Elian — need versus care.** Elian gives the witch an important counterexample to the belief that usefulness proves
affection. He may appreciate something she solves for him. But later he may want her company when nothing needs
solving, or want something from her that cannot be provided through magic. The discovery is not that usefulness is
bad. It is that being needed and being cared for are different relationships.

**Vera — context.** Vera is a counterexample to universal rules about good behaviour. Waiting may help in one
situation and harm in another. Directness may clarify a problem or cause someone to withdraw. Intervention can be
welcome or intrusive. The player must learn the person and the situation rather than discover one universally correct
social policy.

**Ilya — wonder without utility.** Ilya can enjoy a beautiful or ridiculous thing even when it accomplishes nothing.
This character makes room for pointless objects, stories, and small acts of magic that solve no problem. The witch
gradually discovers that an interaction can matter without producing a result.

**Shadow Mirror — witness without capture.** Shadow Mirror is not an all-knowing guide. She has her own history, knows
the Fog, and has left it. She does not diagnose the witch, explain her entire story, or force her toward a conclusion.
She can leave something behind and leave again. Later, the player gradually infers that she knew First Love and that
he entrusted her with something concerning the witch.

The relational system beneath these arcs is deliberately not an affinity meter. It tracks remembered incidents,
expectations, boundaries, access, willingness to share, and local social meaning. Different characters form different
predictions about what the witch will do, and they update those predictions at different rates.

A single surprising action should not magically reset a relationship. Likewise, a disagreement should not
automatically erase care. (Source note: RELATIONAL_SPINE.md.)

The town as a whole serves the same purpose: some people miss what the witch used to do, others prefer new solutions,
some miss her personally, others are relieved not to depend on her, and others barely react. The world does not
deliver one communal verdict on whether she mattered.

That diversity is necessary.

# 8. The magic system: three ways of relating to possibility

Magic should remain gorgeous, satisfying, and powerful. Its transformation concerns its use, not a moral judgment that
magic itself was wrong.

The three conceptual modes in the remembered canon provide a useful structure.

## Violet — I Make

Direct intervention and transformation. The witch changes an object, repairs a mechanism, produces something
improbable, or alters a physical situation. This is the magic she initially loves: the satisfying proof that she can
make something happen.

## Verdigris — I Enable

Create conditions and open possibilities. The magic makes an action possible without dictating which action another
person must choose. Its governing rule is: stabilize the chooser, not the choice. It can support a machine, expose a
constraint, create an opportunity, or provide several people with workable options. It does not have to decide what
anyone should do with those options.

## Gold — I Encounter

Attention without obligation. The final mode is embodied by the ordinary flower: a living thing that is not a puzzle
demanding a solution. The witch may notice it, touch it, and experience its beauty without harvesting, preserving,
cloning, enchanting, or adding it to her reconstructed garden.

These three modes create a philosophical progression without making the protagonist's original magic evil.

At first, agency means changing what exists.

Later, agency also means changing what is possible.

Finally, the player is allowed to encounter something that does not need to become a resource, achievement, or proof.

## The loss of magic

The witch's magic begins to fail as First Love withdraws and her familiar world becomes less predictable. She loses
magical work, money, routine, social centrality, the reliability of the wand, and the confidence that made old actions
feel certain.

That loss should be genuinely difficult. It is not a punishment for being emotionally immature, nor a lesson in which
the game makes magic unpleasant until the witch learns to be a better person.

There should also be no sustained period in which the game withholds magic and compensates with monotonous chores.
Curiosity, character interaction, and environmental discovery must remain rewarding even when the old spells no longer
provide the same answers.

The eventual return of magic is not a prize awarded for correct behaviour. Magic is part of the witch and part of her
world. It returns after her dependence on it as a guarantee of attachment has changed.

# 9. The signature mechanic: learning how a world models you

The game is a Metroidbrainia because knowledge is its most important progression resource. The player does not merely
acquire keys or stronger spells. They learn rules that change what they can understand and do in places they have
already visited.

The core loop is:

NOTICE → PLAY → QUESTION → HYPOTHESIZE → ACT → RESPONSE → COMPARE → REVISE → TRANSFER.

A discovery becomes meaningful when the player can use it to act differently in a later situation.

## Expectation-Sight

The witch has a form of perception called Expectation-Sight. It reveals ghostlike traces of what someone seems to be
bracing for: an anticipated movement, a defensive posture that has not fully happened, a possibility that appears to be
hanging in the space around a character.

It is not mind-reading.

The witch might see someone bracing for a confrontation because she expects one, because that person is worried about
a different event, or because the visual cue is being misinterpreted. False positives are essential. Otherwise,
Expectation-Sight becomes a supernatural truth detector that undermines the entire game.

The player still needs to investigate.

A notebook distinguishes different kinds of information:

| Category       | Meaning                                      |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| SEEN           | Direct visual observation                    |
| HEARD          | Something actually heard                     |
| SHOWN          | Information someone chose to reveal          |
| INFERRED       | An explanation constructed from observations |
| PREDICTED      | What the player expects to happen            |
| ALTERED        | Something changed through an intervention    |
| UNTESTABLE NOW | A question that cannot currently be resolved |

This separation matters because the player must be able to keep a convincing hypothesis without the game upgrading it
into fact.

For example, the player might notice that someone changes the subject whenever the Fog is mentioned. They could infer
that the character knows something about First Love. The inference might be correct, partially correct, or wrong. A
later conversation can change what the earlier behaviour appears to mean.

The notebook is therefore not simply a quest journal. It is the player's workspace for comparing models.

## The Mirror Engine

Every significant action can alter the world and the expectations characters form about the witch.

- SIGNAL: a gesture, sentence, silence, spell, or object interaction.
- INTERPRETATION: someone assigns meaning through their context and history.
- MODEL: their expectations of the witch or another person change—or resist change.
- PREDICTION: they anticipate what will happen next.
- VIOLATION: the witch acts differently than expected.
- REVISION: the model changes, becomes more nuanced, or remains stubborn.
- CONSEQUENCE: access, behaviour, relationships, or the physical world change.

The loop repeats because consequences become new signals.

The engine needs to distinguish four things internally: what actually occurred, what evidence became available, what
each observer believes, and what action they commit to because of that belief. The player sees the resulting
behaviour, not a diagnostic label explaining an NPC's mind.

No trust meters, suspicion percentages, personality quizzes, or universal "correct" social responses.

## The six verbs

The internal authoring ontology is:

- LOOK — examine and compare.
- ASK — invite information or clarification.
- SHOW — disclose evidence, an object, or an idea.
- WAIT — leave space for the world to act without your intervention.
- GO — change position, leave, or accept a transition.
- CAST — change what is physically possible.

These are internal design primitives rather than player-facing verb labels. The interface presents contextual,
natural-language interactions.

The particularly important addition is WAIT. Waiting is not an automatically safe or neutral choice. It can provide
information while allowing someone else to act, leave, change a plan, or influence another character. Observation has
an opportunity cost.

Likewise, CAST can help solve a mechanism while changing the evidence available afterward. If the player modifies a
situation before understanding it, they may get the desired result while losing the opportunity to learn what would
have happened otherwise.

The aim is legible consequences with incomplete access to hidden state. Players should understand why the world
responded as it did without being given complete certainty about every person's mind.

# 10. How puzzles carry the narrative

The puzzles need to progress from concrete physical rules to increasingly complicated questions about people, memory,
and evidence.

They should never become arbitrary metaphors in which an emotional revelation magically unlocks a door because the
designer wants the player to understand a theme.

Instead, the thematic insight should be a consequence of the puzzle's actual structure.

## The progression of player knowledge

1. Perceptual literacy. Notice details that appear unimportant, distinguish different objects, and recognize
   discrepancies in a scene.
2. Causal literacy. Experiment with mechanisms, magic, and ordinary materials; learn how specific changes produce
   specific results.
3. Social literacy. Discover that a technically successful solution can still conflict with someone's preferences or
   authorship.
4. Temporal literacy. Wait, revisit, compare routines, and learn that events continue outside the player's immediate
   view.
5. Semantic literacy. Distinguish an event from its interpretation, and an interpretation from the evidence that
   supports it.
6. Epistemic literacy. Learn that different models can explain the same observations and that some questions remain
   unresolved.
7. Relational literacy. Choose actions that acknowledge what another person may want while leaving their choice
   genuinely open.
8. Commitment under uncertainty. Act when understanding is sufficient, without demanding that every unknown be
   settled first.

## The signature puzzle: the unsrescuable rescue

The player discovers a genuine magical procedure capable of retrieving First Love from the Fog.

It works.

This is critical. The game must not reveal that the witch was incapable of succeeding all along. The challenge is not
to find a secret reason the spell fails. The player must distinguish the result a spell produces from the meaning they
hope that result establishes.

The retrieval spell can change someone's location or state. But if the witch brings First Love back by force, his
return does not establish that he freely chose to return.

The procedure can satisfy the literal objective and destroy the very evidence the witch wants.

That is a logical contradiction, not a morality quiz. The player should reach it through previous experiments
involving objects, willing participants, locations, altered states, and magical echoes.

The game's deeper solution is understanding why the apparently correct solution cannot deliver the desired knowledge.

The story should not then reward the player with a canonical reunion, a secret good ending, or a speech confirming that
they understood the lesson. First Love's departure remains real.

## The anti-puzzle: the Garden that cannot be saved

Later, the player witnesses the Garden's decay as magic collapses.

The tempting objective is to save it. But the player has already learned to distinguish solvable anomalies from
irreversible consequences.

There should be no hidden item or perfectly timed spell that restores the Garden.

The discovery is that the Garden is not simply a machine waiting to be repaired. Its decay records a change that has
happened to the world.

The player is asked to understand what is happening, rather than reverse it.

## The impossible final event

The final mystery should also remain partly unsolved, but not because the developers have abandoned coherence. It
should be supported by several plausible interpretations with enough evidence to make each reasonable, while leaving
one crucial element unresolved.

The player can build a model. They can make a commitment. They cannot obtain total certainty first.

This is the final progression of the Metroidbrainia: not merely possessing more knowledge, but knowing what knowledge
can and cannot do. (Source note: MYTHIC_SPINE.md.)

# 11. Full narrative outline

The story has two connected movements: the upper loop of mastery and the lower loop of integration. The protagonist
first attempts to solve uncertainty by becoming more capable. She then discovers why capability alone cannot resolve
the problem, returns to the living world, and learns to use what she has without making it the condition of
belonging.

## Prologue — A witch at full power

The opening establishes the witch as radiant, competent, funny, and fun to inhabit.

She makes improbable things happen. People know her. Her home and workshop contain evidence of a busy, inventive life.
The wand, violet hood, magic, routines, and reputation reinforce a coherent identity.

The player should genuinely enjoy being her.

The father belongs to the earlier emotional history, but that history should initially arrive through fragments: a
drawing, an object, an old message, a remembered gesture, or something that looks ordinary until later. The game must
not begin by diagnosing the protagonist or explaining that her personality is a protective strategy.

The opening also establishes the Original Garden, which the witch and First Love built together. It is imperfect and
genuinely shared. The two have different preferences; neither person's contribution can be removed without changing
the place.

The player learns to look, touch, experiment, cast, combine, and laugh at unexpected but coherent consequences.

The initial emotional promise is simple:

I love being this witch. I love what I can make happen.

## Act I — The person who begins to leave

First Love starts spending time in the Fog.

At first, he returns. Then he visits more often, hides some of his visits, stays longer, and becomes increasingly
difficult to reach. The player observes these changes without receiving a definitive explanation for them.

The witch tries to regain her footing through action. She investigates, repairs, anticipates, and uses her magic to
keep the situation from slipping out of her control.

The player learns the difference between a prediction and an explanation. Predicting when First Love will visit the
Fog does not establish why he wants to go there.

The departure eventually happens.

The witch's immediate interpretation is not simply that she misses him. It is that another place has displaced her:
she has been replaced by something she cannot compete with.

Her response is to become more determined to solve the problem.

## Act II — The world discovers substitutes

The witch's magic begins failing.

The change is material as well as emotional. Magical work becomes unreliable. Money and routine become less
dependable. The town no longer requires her for every task she once performed.

Other people make adaptations. A mechanism is repaired differently. Someone develops an alternative to a magical
service. An old dependency disappears. A person who once waited for the witch begins doing things independently.

The same change produces different responses in different characters. Some miss the witch's abilities. Some prefer the
new arrangements. Some miss her personally. Some do not particularly notice.

The witch sees a function being replaced and hears a verdict on her own identity.

This is the first major reinterpretation of the story's central question. The player must begin to distinguish
something I used to do from who I am to someone.

Her basement becomes the next frontier because the practical world no longer gives her the certainty she expects. Old
materials and tools lead into a place that is impossible in scale and logic.

The underworld begins inside the ordinary world. There is no simple portal that cleanly separates normal life from an
adventure realm.

## Act III — The Garden below

The player descends into the basement and discovers that its space is governed by strange but learnable rules.

Rooms may repeat, distort, or change in response to how the player understands them. Repeated action without model
revision can create loops. Remembered rooms can diverge from rooms that were actually lived in. Insisting that a space
must be exactly as remembered may make it rigid; acknowledging that memory could be wrong may open possibilities.

The player discovers the Garden Below.

At first, it looks like a miracle. The garden can be reconstructed and preserved. It appears to offer a way around
separation: if the original relationship cannot be maintained, perhaps its world can be kept intact.

But the player notices that the reconstruction contains only the witch's preferred flowers. First Love's preferences
have vanished. Shared imperfections and contradictions have been erased. The Garden is too still.

The player recognizes that the witch has preserved her representation of the relationship, not the relationship as a
living space created by two independent people.

It is beautiful. It is also insufficient.

This matters because the game must not mock the wish to preserve something. Remembering and keeping objects are not
inherently wrong. The problem is mistaking preservation for participation.

## Act IV — The wand and the choice

The wand crisis is the hinge of the game.

The wand is the physical object most closely bound to the witch's exceptional identity. It is beloved, useful, and
magical; it has become overloaded with the promise that power must secure attachment.

The player can confront the wand through several possible actions: shatter it, keep it, give it away, or hide it. The
choice is not an inventory optimization problem, and no option should secretly be the morally correct answer.

Each action should leave different material traces and callbacks. The critical point is the commitment the player
makes about the object and its meaning.

The witch reaches the familiar defensive phrase:

«Ну и не надо».

She can declare she no longer needs the wand, cling to it, pass it to someone else, or hide it from view. None of these
actions instantly settles what the wand means to her.

What must change is her relationship to the guarantee she once associated with it: the belief that exceptional power
must secure attachment.

The wand is not evil. Magic is not the enemy. What has been placed under examination is the demand that power produce
a particular relational outcome.

## Act V — The unsrescuable rescue

The deepest part of the underworld offers a real route to retrieve First Love from the Fog.

The player has learned enough physical and magical rules to execute the procedure. Its success is not withheld by a
contrived failure.

Instead, the player has to understand the distinction between bringing someone back and knowing they freely chose to
return.

Forcing the result would not answer the relational question. It could even erase the evidence that would have
answered it.

The game does not demand that the player agree with an abstract moral rule. It asks the player to recognize the limits
of the solution they have discovered.

The Garden then decays as the magic sustaining it collapses. There is no hidden rescue puzzle. The structures may
remain for a time, but the beauty drains away, and eventually only dust remains.

The player cannot restore the shared relationship by constructing a sufficiently accurate copy.

The protagonist returns to the living world without First Love, without her previous status, and without the old
certainty that magical power can hold everything in place.

This is a return without restoration.

## Act VI — Learning to live among independent people

The witch returns to town. The relationships are still there, but they have not automatically become harmonious.

Tomas continues to care about authorship. Elian still distinguishes need from care. Vera still responds according to
context. Ilya still enjoys things that do not need to accomplish anything. The raccoon remains brilliant,
inconsistent, funny, and independent.

The witch must now act in the same world with a more flexible model of it.

She can ask before intervening, tolerate refusal, show evidence without making the decision for someone else, allow
another person to finish a project, accept disappointment, and distinguish a request for help from an obligation to fix
everything.

But the game should not turn these into universal virtues. Sometimes direct intervention is appropriate. Sometimes
waiting is harmful. Sometimes the right thing is to leave someone alone; sometimes it is to stay. The player must
understand the particular situation rather than follow a formula.

The town's response to the witch's absence continues to be differentiated. Its adaptations show that losing a function
is not equivalent to erasing a person.

The player learns that a more accurate model of another person does not mean a completely transparent model.

## Act VII — Magic returns

The witch's magic returns in full.

The moment should be beautiful and joyful. The player should feel the pleasure of wielding a great capability again,
not the relief of escaping a punishment.

But the meanings of her actions can now expand.

Violet magic can still make and transform. Verdigris magic can enable without dictating. The player discovers that
power can reveal constraints, provide support, create access, and open options for other people instead of always
finishing the job on their behalf.

The witch does not stop being a witch. She no longer has to prove her right to exist by performing at her peak every
time someone needs something.

The raccoon reaches a parallel point in his own story. He can show an imperfect invention without making it a promise
or a verdict on his ability.

These are not triumphant speeches about growth. They are changed actions in ordinary situations.

## Act VIII — The ordinary flower

The witch encounters an ordinary, imperfect flower.

It is not a treasure or a quest item. It does not need to be picked, preserved, cloned, enchanted, or used to repair
the Garden Below.

She can simply encounter it.

This is the arrival of Gold — I Encounter: an event whose value is not dependent on turning it into something else.

The flower is important precisely because the game does not force it to become important through utility. It exists in
the world without needing the witch's permission or intervention.

The player should be allowed to look, touch, and move on.

No reward pop-up should explain what the flower means.

## Act IX — The invitation

Something impossible happens.

The player has enough evidence to construct a plausible account of it but not enough to determine every detail
conclusively. The event must remain compatible with the world's established rules, even as it challenges the player's
current model.

Shadow Mirror appears.

She says:

> "Hey."

Then:

> "Come on."

There is no demand, no explanation that resolves everything, and no guarantee that the invitation leads to a
particular outcome.

The witch can go.

She does.

The camera stays.

The mystery remains.

This is the final transformation of her old behaviour. She once tried to make people stay. Now someone invites her
onward, and she chooses to follow without first obtaining proof of what the invitation means.

The player does not need to finish solving the mystery to unlock the protagonist's decision.

The game proper ends here.

# 12. The postgame: the player looks back

After the protagonist leaves, the player can continue exploring the house.

This is a structural inversion of the underworld descent. During the main game, the protagonist was compelled to
descend because she wanted to recover what was lost. In the postgame, the player can descend voluntarily, after the
protagonist has already chosen to continue living.

The house is not a frozen memorial. It is lived-in and active. Objects have acquired new uses. There are gifts,
unfinished projects, traces of the raccoon, evidence of relationships, and remnants of ordinary life.

The player becomes an archaeologist of relationships.

## The gift mystery

The postgame gradually establishes that Shadow Mirror knew First Love.

The evidence is distributed through repeated motifs and physical traces: a familiar knot, an unusual material
signature, a private object, an old joke, a flower, or an object Shadow Mirror could not plausibly possess without a
prior connection.

The player reconstructs the relationships between these objects, their histories, and the people who made or received
them.

The eventual inference is that First Love asked Shadow Mirror to care for the witch.

This is an important truth, but it is not a complete explanation.

The player does not recover the exact conversation. They do not learn the complete motive for leaving the Fog. They do
not receive a hidden canonical answer that cancels what happened.

The final evidence supports several things simultaneously:

- He left.
- He chose the Fog.
- He cared about the witch.

One does not cancel another.

The revelation adds a new meaning to earlier events. Shadow Mirror's objects and actions were not random; they were
evidence of a connection the player had not yet understood.

This is the ultimate Metroidbrainia reward: reinterpretation. The player has acquired no new spell. They have learned
to recognize relationships in evidence that was present all along.

The protagonist has already gone. The player is allowed to keep looking.

That is the final division of agency: the witch's story is about acting without complete certainty, while the player's
postgame is about investigating without being promised a final answer. (Source note: MYTHIC_SPINE.md.)

# 13. The aesthetic and tonal identity

The target visual language is The Witch, The Raccoon, and the Polygonal Wonderland: an authored, crooked 3D world that
feels like an impossible childhood memory of late-1990s and early-2000s adventure games.

The reference synthesis is Grim Fandango's theatrical staging, Monkey Island's comic world logic, the expressive
absurdity of Gravity Falls, the approachable adventure-game sensibility of Humongous Entertainment, and the tactile
oddness of hand-built miniature worlds.

The objective is not to imitate an old game. It is to recreate the emotional feeling of a crooked, handmade 3D world
using a contemporary production pipeline.

## The art-direction rules

The geometry should be visibly authored: wedges, cubes, slabs, cones, uneven surfaces, exaggerated silhouettes, and
slightly awkward proportions. Textures should feel painted rather than physically accurate. Lighting should establish
theatrical focus, with warm pools, violet shadows, fog, and strong staging.

The fixed-camera 2.5D presentation should give scenes the clarity of illustrated theatre sets. A room is not an
interchangeable box containing objects; it is a composed visual sentence with a dominant question, a clear focal point,
secondary landmarks, and details that reward further attention.

The world should have enough physical depth to feel like a place rather than a flat illustration. Clutter, rooflines,
attics, workshops, balconies, basements, and impossible vertical arrangements help the environments feel inhabited.

The visual progression should echo the narrative without simply colour-coding the witch's emotional state. The upper
world is rich and tactile; the lower world preserves beauty while making its underlying assumptions unstable; the
return restores spectacle without restoring the old certainty.

## The tonal contract

The target tone is:

Deep whimsy, tactile wonder, absurdist comedy, warmth, mystery, melancholy, and curiosity.

Comedy is a causal system, not decoration. Strange actions should produce unexpected but coherent results. Failed
experiments should often be funny and informative. Characters' habits, peculiar priorities, and misunderstandings
should generate humour through the world itself.

The game should make room for genuine emotional pain without turning every scene into drama. A ridiculous object can
remain ridiculous. A character can be exhausting and lovable in the same conversation. A puzzle can be difficult
without humiliating the player.

The rule is not that everything secretly symbolizes something sad. The rule is that the world is allowed to contain
both genuine whimsy and genuine sadness without either invalidating the other.

# 14. The actual player experience

The game's ideal experience has two intertwined loops.

## The adventure loop

Notice → Experiment → Discover a rule → Apply it elsewhere.

The player grows more capable because they understand more of the world's causal and semantic structure.

## The relational loop

Form a hypothesis → Act on it → Observe a response → Revise or defend.

The player learns that other people have independent goals and that the meaning of an action depends on who
interprets it.

The combination is what gives the game its identity.

A conventional point-and-click adventure usually asks the player to infer what object, action, or piece of information
will solve the current problem. Witch you were hear adds a second question: what is the problem actually about, and
what will solving it mean to the person involved?

A machine can be physically broken and socially sensitive. A character may possess the information needed to solve a
mystery while being unwilling to share it. An intervention can improve a situation while damaging the conditions
needed to understand it.

The game should not make these outcomes random. They emerge from authored rules, observable evidence, prior actions,
and individual differences.

## Interface and accessibility

The game is mobile-first, with desktop as a secondary platform. Interaction should be contextual and expressed in
natural language rather than forcing players to memorize an abstract verb taxonomy.

The player should always have a plausible next thing to try, even if the answer is not immediately obvious. Wrong
hypotheses should usually produce new evidence instead of a hard reset. Hinting should help the player notice
overlooked information before revealing the answer outright.

Most importantly, the player must be allowed to experiment without the game treating curiosity as a mistake.

# 15. The production architecture

The technical architecture should support the narrative principles rather than become a separate, unnecessarily
complicated simulation project.

The remembered stack is Godot 4.6.3, Popochiu 2.1.1, and Dialogue Manager 3.10.4. The intended design is a
point-and-click adventure whose authored interactions feed a deterministic, stateful model of the world.

The core path is:

1. Contextual player interaction: natural-language choices, object interactions, movement, waiting.
2. Popochiu / MirrorAction: resolve the interaction into an authored action.
3. World and relationship resolver: apply world rules, expectations, knowledge, and constraints.
4. EventLog and transaction queue: record causes, control state changes, prevent reentrancy problems.
5. Observable consequences: world state, dialogue, routines, access, object changes, new evidence.

The Mirror Engine should maintain clear boundaries between actual events, available evidence, player hypotheses, NPC
beliefs, and public or communal interpretations. These should not be collapsed into one flag that says what the story
is "really about."

State transitions should be deterministic and testable. The EventLog records what happened and why; the resolver
determines what changes; characters respond according to their own state and history.

StateCharts can coordinate narrative modes and prevent reentrant transitions. Dialogue Manager acts as an authored
dialogue layer rather than the owner of the entire relationship system. LimboAI, if used, should remain confined to
operational behaviour and blackboard data rather than becoming a second source of narrative truth.

The goal is not to simulate the full complexity of human psychology. It is to build a bounded, authored world whose
responses are rich enough to support surprising but understandable discoveries.

Production tests should verify:

- world rules remain consistent;
- different observers can interpret the same event differently;
- actions change the relevant state and nothing unrelated;
- incorrect hypotheses yield useful evidence;
- no critical puzzle becomes unsolvable through an accidental state combination;
- revisiting a place can produce a meaningful new interpretation;
- the narrative never unintentionally turns an ambiguous belief into established fact.

That is the technical implementation of the project's governing law: legible consequence, incomplete access to hidden
state.

# 16. What must never be sacrificed

The project has accumulated many useful ideas. The danger is that the game becomes an exhibition of clever systems
instead of a coherent experience.

I would treat these as the non-negotiable design invariants.

- **Magic remains beautiful.** The story is not an argument against power or ambition.
- **The protagonist remains fun.** She must retain her wit, generosity, theatricality, inventiveness, and occasional
  ridiculousness.
- **First Love genuinely chooses.** The Fog is not simply evil, and his departure is not invalidated by a hidden final
  explanation.
- **The rescue is technically possible.** The central puzzle concerns what success establishes, not a contrived reason
  the spell cannot work.
- **The Garden cannot be restored as the original relationship.** A preserved representation cannot substitute for a
  shared life.
- **Characters retain independent agency.** No NPC exists solely to reveal the protagonist's psychology or validate a
  moral conclusion.
- **No universal social solution.** Waiting, helping, directness, restraint, and intervention depend on the person and
  context.
- **Magic returns without restoring the old arrangement.** Growth changes what the witch does with power, not whether
  she is allowed to have power.
- **The ending requires no total explanation.** The witch can choose to go without proof, while the player can
  investigate afterward.
- **The postgame adds meaning, not a secret exoneration.** The gift mystery reveals care and connection without erasing
  departure or restoring the relationship.

These constraints protect the game against becoming a conventional story about a protagonist who discovers that her
special ability was bad and has to become ordinary.

That is not this game.

Its emotional power comes from the opposite proposition: her talent is extraordinary, her desire is legitimate, and her
love of magic is worth preserving. What she must relinquish is the demand that these things secure another person's
choice.

# 17. The unresolved continuity decisions

A synthesis is not complete if it silently converts every unfinished idea into a supposedly settled fact. These are the
decisions I would carry forward explicitly.

First: the father and First Love. The remembered backstory identifies the father's early loving presence and later
departure as the source of the replacement fear. The narrative and relational spines separately develop First Love,
who leaves for the Fog. My synthesis treats the father's departure as the earlier history and First Love as the
present-day relational crisis. That preserves both strands without merging them. The final canon should make their
relationship to one another unmistakable without reducing either character to a device.

Second: the exact rules of the Fog. Its emotional and experiential function is clear: it offers an alternative way of
being and is not merely evil. The precise mechanics, origins, and complete motive for choosing it should remain
unresolved until they can be established through coherent world rules and authored evidence.

Third: the wand's branches. The player can make different choices about the wand, but the narrative must keep the
hinge intact. Each branch should have real local consequences and callbacks, while the later return of magic remains
compatible with the central principle that the wand itself was never the enemy.

Fourth: the scope of the final impossible event. It should be carefully authored enough that multiple interpretations
are genuinely supported. The unresolved element should be deliberate, not a loose end produced by insufficient
explanation.

Fifth: the relationship between title and story. Witch you were hear should retain its deliberate spelling. Its
connection to being heard, intimacy, memory, and presence can resonate through the game, but the title should not
force every plot point to become an auditory metaphor.

These are the areas where a strong high-level structure still needs exact authored scenes and tested implementation
decisions.

# 18. The final synthesis

The game begins by giving the player extraordinary capability.

They can cast spells, discover mechanisms, fix impossible things, and learn the logic of a world that responds to
their curiosity. Their first instinct is to solve problems and make things happen.

Then the world begins to reveal its independence.

A person leaves. The town adapts. A machine can be repaired in more than one way. The Garden can be preserved without
being alive. A rescue can succeed without establishing free choice. A relationship can leave traces without being
restored.

The witch gradually discovers that power is not the same as control, that care is not the same as takeover, and that
being needed is not the same as being chosen.

But she is never asked to become less magical, less inventive, or less herself.

She learns to act without requiring the world to provide a particular verdict on her worth.

At the end, Shadow Mirror offers an invitation. The witch does not first demand to know exactly what it means, what
will happen, or whether she can guarantee the outcome.

She goes.

The camera stays behind. The player remains free to inspect the evidence that still exists, discover a relationship
that was hidden in plain sight, and arrive at a more nuanced interpretation of the past.

The player can understand more than the protagonist did. But even then, they do not get everything.

That is the central design achievement to aim for: the player's increasing understanding should improve their
capacity to act, not eliminate every uncertainty before they act.

The deepest promise of Witch you were hear is not that every mystery will be explained, every relationship restored,
or every loss reversed. It is that the world will remain worth exploring when certainty fails—and that the player can
learn to participate in it without needing to own what happens next.
